\begin{abstract}
Steering vectors offer a cheap way to alter language-model behavior at inference time, but
composing two behaviors by simply adding their vectors is unreliable. We ask a diagnostic question:
when two already-steerable behavior directions are added, does their geometry predict what happens?
We study eight persona and style directions in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and evaluate all $28$ pairs
under two controlled composition schemes. We separate each joint outcome into a \emph{behavioral
magnitude}, how much behavior is expressed, and a \emph{directional residual}, whether adding the
second vector reinforces or suppresses expression relative to steering each behavior alone. Because
the same behaviors recur across many pairs, we assess significance with exact behavior-level
permutations rather than treating pairs as independent. We find that signed cosine predicts the directional
residual in both schemes ($\beta=0.31$ and $0.41$), while magnitude effects have smaller
coefficients ($\beta\approx0.19$) and are more sensitive to the injection control and permutation
structure. The result is therefore not a universal composition rule, but a moderate geometric
tendency: vector orientation is most informative about reinforcement versus suppression, conditional
on one model, one layer, an LLM judge, and sparse coverage of strongly opposed behavior pairs.
\end{abstract}

%=========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=========================================================================

Activation steering aims to change a language model's behavior at inference time by adding a
vector to an internal activation. Prior work shows that contrastively extracted directions can
induce behaviors such as style, sentiment, refusal, or persona-like traits without retraining
\citep{turner2023actadd,zou2023repe,panickssery2024caa,chen2025persona}. This makes steering
vectors attractive not only as controls but also as probes: if behaviors are approximately linear
directions in activation space, then their pairwise geometry should say something about how they
interact \citep{park2024linear}.

The difficulty is that multi-behavior steering is not reliably additive. A practitioner may want to
make a model, for example, both more formal and more humorous by adding two vectors. In practice,
naive addition can preserve one behavior while weakening the other, amplify both, or degrade text
entirely \citep{vanderweij2024extending}. Existing work has mostly responded constructively, by
searching for better composition operators or more robust steering procedures, including layer-wise
multi-vector injection, conceptor-based steering matrices, dynamic vector composition, and nonlinear
multi-attribute control \citep{vanderweij2024extending,postmus2024conceptorsteering,han2026steer2adapt,oozeer2025beyond}. We instead ask the
simpler diagnostic question: \emph{under additive composition, what aspect of the joint outcome is
predicted by the geometry of the two directions?} This diagnostic question is significant because recent composition methods suggest that additive
failure has multiple possible causes: the vectors may need to be injected at different layers
\citep{vanderweij2024extending}, a single direction may be too poor a representation of a behaviour
\citep{postmus2024conceptorsteering}, the right coefficients may be task-dependent
\citep{han2026steer2adapt}, or the control surface may be nonlinear
\citep{oozeer2025beyond}. Our goal is to isolate what can already be explained before introducing
any of these additional mechanisms. By fixing the operator to addition and varying only the geometry
of the two directions, we ask whether pairwise geometry diagnoses a scale problem, a directional
compatibility problem, or neither. This makes additive composition a
controlled probe of what more sophisticated composition methods must ultimately handle.

Our answer is a decomposition. The total amount of behavior expressed under joint steering and the
sign of the compositional change are different quantities. We therefore measure both: a
\emph{magnitude} outcome, which asks how large the joint behavioral movement is, and a
\emph{directional residual}, which asks whether the second vector reinforces or suppresses each
behavior relative to injecting that behavior alone. To avoid mistaking geometry for raw steering
power, we control for \emph{single-vector strength}: how strongly each behavior is activated when its
own vector is injected by itself. To make our results more robust, we also evaluate two normalizations of the composed perturbation,
one fixing its total norm and one fixing its projection onto each behavior direction. Across all
pairs of eight validated directions, the signed cosine between directions predicts the directional
residual under both normalization schemes. Magnitude has a smaller controlled coefficient and a less
direct interpretation, because it is more entangled with perturbation size and coherence failures.
Therefore, for this model and behavior set, geometry is more informative
about reinforcement versus suppression than about absolute joint expression.

\section{Related Work}
%=========================================================================

\paragraph{Activation steering and behavior directions.}
Activation Addition, Contrastive Activation Addition, Representation Engineering, and related
inference-time interventions extract or learn directions in activation space and add them during a
forward pass to shift model behavior \citep{turner2023actadd,panickssery2024caa,zou2023repe,li2023iti}.
We follow the response-averaged, difference-of-means construction used by Persona Vectors
\citep{chen2025persona}, which is well suited to persona and style traits. Recent work also cautions
that steerability is not automatic: effectiveness varies across behaviors, prompts, and examples
\citep{tan2024analysing,imli2025unified}. We therefore condition our study on directions that are
individually steerable under a validation protocol.

\paragraph{Composition, reliability, and geometry.}
Naive composition of steering vectors is known to be fragile, motivating alternatives to plain
summation. These include placing individual vectors at different layers, replacing vectors with
conceptor or projection-like steering matrices, dynamically composing reusable vector bases, and
computing nonlinear multi-attribute interventions from classifiers
\citep{vanderweij2024extending,postmus2024conceptorsteering,han2026steer2adapt,oozeer2025beyond}.
Those works ask how to compose better. We ask when the simplest composition is predictable. This
question is naturally geometric: if behavior directions are approximately linear, then the angle
between two objectives should affect whether they reinforce or trade off \citep{park2024linear,young2026alignmenttax}.
Our contribution is empirical and diagnostic: we test whether pairwise vector geometry predicts a
measured compositional residual, while controlling for the fact that some single vectors are simply
more effective than others.

\section{Methods}
%=========================================================================

\paragraph{Behaviors and directions.}
We study eight behaviors: \emph{apathetic}, \emph{confidence}, \emph{evil}, \emph{formality},
\emph{hallucinating}, \emph{humorous}, \emph{impolite}, and \emph{sycophantic}. Six behaviours, and the prompts used to elicit them, are taken from
the released Persona Vectors trait set, and two (\emph{confidence} and \emph{formality}) are generated
with the same prompt template. The set spans persona and stylistic register, and was constrained by
compute and by the requirement that each direction be individually steerable under our validation
protocol.

For each behavior $b$, we compute a residual-stream direction by contrasting responses generated
under a behavior-inducing system prompt with responses generated under a matched neutral prompt.
Let $A_b^+$ and $A_b^-$ be the retained positive and neutral activation sets, where each activation
is the response-token average of the residual stream at a fixed layer $L^\star$. The direction is
\begin{equation}
  v_b = \frac{1}{|A_b^+|}\sum_{a\in A_b^+}a
      - \frac{1}{|A_b^-|}\sum_{a\in A_b^-}a,
  \qquad
  \hat v_b = v_b/\lVert v_b\rVert .
  \label{eq:direction}
\end{equation}
All experiments use \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}. We intervene at $L^\star=17$ in
our code convention, corresponding to the residual stream after transformer block $16$ in the
32-block stack. The perturbation is added only to generated response tokens, not to prompt tokens.

\paragraph{Composition schemes.}
For a pair $(i,j)$, write the cosine similarity $c_{ij}=\hat v_i^\top \hat v_j$ and $s=\hat v_i+\hat v_j$. We evaluate two
controlled additive schemes. \emph{Normalized-sum} injection fixes the total perturbation norm,
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\mathrm{norm}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{norm}}\frac{s}{\lVert s\rVert},
  \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{norm}}=4.5.
  \label{eq:normsum}
\end{equation}
We chose $\alpha_{\mathrm{norm}}=4.5$ by a small search over normalized-sum strengths, selecting the
best effect--coherence tradeoff, and then used the same value for the projection-controlled scheme
for comparability (Appendix~\ref{app:composition-strength}). \emph{Projection-controlled} injection fixes
the projection of the perturbation onto each behavior direction,
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\mathrm{axis}} = \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{axis}}}{1+c_{ij}}s,
  \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{axis}}=4.5.
  \label{eq:axis}
\end{equation}
This second scheme controls one notion of dose but not all of them: its total norm is
$\lVert\delta_{\mathrm{axis}}\rVert=\alpha_{\mathrm{axis}}\sqrt{2/(1+c_{ij})}$, so for opposed pairs the model can
receive a larger total perturbation. We analyze the
resulting coherence failures are as composition failures.

\paragraph{Outcomes.}
For each pair and scheme we run four settings: no steering $(0,0)$, steering $i$ alone $(1,0)$,
steering $j$ alone $(0,1)$, and joint steering $(1,1)$. Let $E_i^{(a,b)}$ be the mean judge score for
behavior $i$ under setting $(a,b)$. We define the single-vector and joint effects for behavior $i$ as
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_i^{\mathrm{single}}=E_i^{(1,0)}-E_i^{(0,0)},
  \qquad
  \Delta_i^{\mathrm{joint}}=E_i^{(1,1)}-E_i^{(0,0)},
\end{equation}
with analogous quantities for $j$. The magnitude outcome is
\begin{equation}
  M(i,j)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_i^{\mathrm{joint}}+\Delta_j^{\mathrm{joint}}\right),
  \label{eq:mag}
\end{equation}
and the directional residual is
\begin{equation}
  S(i,j)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(\Delta_i^{\mathrm{joint}}-\Delta_i^{\mathrm{single}})
  +(\Delta_j^{\mathrm{joint}}-\Delta_j^{\mathrm{single}})\right].
  \label{eq:directional}
\end{equation}
Positive $S$ means joint steering reinforces the behaviors relative to steering them alone; negative
$S$ means suppression. This quantity asks whether adding the second vector raises or lowers the
score obtained from the first vector alone, averaged symmetrically over the two behaviors. To avoid mistaking single-vector strength for composition, all confirmatory
models control for both the mean and the maximum of
$|\Delta_i^{\mathrm{single}}|$ and $|\Delta_j^{\mathrm{single}}|$.

\section{Experiments}
%=========================================================================

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
For every pair, scheme, and setting, we generate $20$ prompts with $5$ samples per prompt
($100$ generations), using temperature $1.0$, top-$p=1.0$, and a maximum of $600$ new tokens. We
score behavior and coherence with a blinded LLM judge, \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, following the Persona
Vectors \citep{chen2025persona} protocol: each call sees only the question and answer, and returns a probability-weighted
mean over integer scores from $0$ to $100$. The coherence judge is independent of the behavior
rubrics. Validation of the LLM judge is done by checking agreement between LLM judge scores and human annotated ones (\textcolor{red}{$n$, agreement metric, and agreement value to insert}), as reported in Appendix ~\ref{app:judge}.

\paragraph{Coherence gate.}
Large activation interventions can make generations repetitive or malformed, making behavior
scores unreliable. We therefore analyze behavior only for pairs whose joint generations remain
coherent: a pair is retained if its mean coherence over the $100$ joint samples is at least $30$.
This gate removes no normalized-sum pairs and six projection-controlled pairs. These removals are
not random: five of the six have $c_{ij}\leq 0.13$, as expected from the total-norm inflation of
Eq.~\ref{eq:axis} at low cosine. Thus, projection-controlled results should be read on the retained
support, while normalized-sum covers the full observed range $c_{ij}\in[-0.52,0.70]$.

\paragraph{Relational-data inference.}
The $28$ behavior pairs are not independent observations: each behavior appears in seven pairs, so
pairs sharing a behavior also share part of their geometric and behavioral measurements. We therefore
use a dyadic, or relational-data, inference procedure rather than treating the $28$ pair measurements
as independent samples. For each scheme, we fit standardized regressions of $M$ or $S$ on $c_{ij}$,
controlling for mean and maximum single-vector strength, and compute significance by exact
behavior-level permutation.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main result. Signed cosine predicts the directional residual in both
controlled schemes. The effect is moderate rather than deterministic: the normalized-sum coefficient
is $+0.310$ with exact $p=0.047$, and the projection-controlled coefficient is $+0.411$ with
$p<0.001$ under the masked node-permutation null. The interpretation is that aligned directions tend
to reinforce each other relative to single-vector steering, while opposed directions tend to
suppress.

Two qualifications are important. First, the evidence favors signed orientation over unsigned
similarity. Replacing $c_{ij}$ by $|c_{ij}|$ attenuates the
directional coefficient strongly in both schemes, especially under normalized-sum, but the
projection-controlled estimate remains significant. Second, magnitude
is not null: its controlled coefficient is small in both schemes ($\beta\approx0.19$), but the
projection-controlled permutation test assigns it a very small $p$-value. Thus, the result is not that geometry affects only direction. Rather, its clearest role is in
predicting reinforcement versus suppression.

Appendix~\ref{app:stats} and Appendix~\ref{app:coherence} support the same cautious reading:
semantic similarity does not explain the directional association, but leave-one-behavior-out and
coherence-gating checks show that the effect oscillated between behavior-sets. We therefore read it as a
moderate tendency, not a sharp rule.

\section{Conclusion}
%=========================================================================

We studied whether the geometry of two steering vectors predicts the outcome of additive behavior
composition. Using eight individually steerable persona and style directions in
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, we evaluated every behavior pair under normalized-sum and
projection-controlled joint injection. We separated each outcome into a magnitude term, measuring
how much behavior is expressed under joint steering, and a directional residual, measuring whether
adding the second vector reinforces or suppresses behavior relative to steering that behavior alone.
Because behavior pairs are not independent, we used exact node-permutation tests rather than
treating pairs as independent.

The main empirical finding is that signed cosine is predictive of the directional residual in both
controlled schemes. Aligned directions tend to reinforce one another, while opposed directions tend
to suppress. Magnitude also shows geometric association in some analyses, but with smaller
coefficients and a less direct interpretation because magnitude is more entangled with single-vector
strength, perturbation size, and the coherence gate. Thus the paper's narrow conclusion is that
vector orientation is most useful for predicting the sign of compositional change, not for providing
a complete rule for how strongly two behaviors will jointly appear.

The study has several limitations. It uses one model, one layer, one behavior set, and an LLM judge
whose human-validation numbers must be inserted before submission. Only seven of the $28$ behavior
pairs have negative cosine, so the suppression side is thinly sampled. The projection-controlled
scheme censors several low-cosine pairs through coherence collapse, making its permutation null
unusual and requiring the very small $p$-values to be read alongside effect sizes. Finally, our
residual $S$ is operational: it measures change relative to single-vector steering, but it is not a
full factorial interaction that subtracts cross-expression from the other vector alone.

Future work should first broaden the behavior set to populate the strongly opposed regime and test
whether the same directional pattern survives with more negative-cosine pairs. A second priority is
to compute stricter interaction quantities that subtract cross-expression, and to validate the
judge-derived outcomes against larger human-labeled samples. Finally, repeating the analysis across
models, layers, and composition operators would clarify whether this geometric tendency is a
property of these particular directions or a more general feature of activation-space behavior
composition.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:impl}

All experiments use \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}, which has $32$ transformer blocks and
hidden size $4096$. Layer indexing in code is zero-indexed for blocks, while hidden states follow
the Hugging Face convention: $L^\star=17$ refers to \texttt{output\_hidden\_states[17]}, the residual
stream after block $16$. The hook is registered on \texttt{model.model.layers[16]} and writes into
the block output, i.e. post-attention, post-MLP, after residual addition. Steering is applied only to
response positions during autoregressive decoding; prompt tokens are not perturbed. During
generation, the hook adds the perturbation to the newly generated token at each step.

Generation uses sampling with temperature $1.0$, top-$p=1.0$, no top-$k$, and
\texttt{max\_new\_tokens}=600. There is no early stop criterion other than the maximum token budget.
Each pair/scheme/setting condition has $20$ prompts and $5$ samples per prompt, for $100$
generations. Each pair and scheme has four settings: baseline, first single-vector injection, second
single-vector injection, and joint injection.

Single-vector strength was swept over $\alpha\in\{2,4,6,8\}$ on unit-normalized directions at
$L^\star=17$, with $\alpha=3$ additionally probed for a separate trait-specific check. The selected
single-vector operating point was $\alpha=4$, chosen as a knee of the effect--coherence tradeoff
across validated traits, with mean trait change $+45.6$ and mean coherence $77.7$. Composition uses
$\alpha_{\mathrm{norm}}=4.5$ for normalized-sum and $\alpha_{\mathrm{axis}}=4.5$ for
projection-controlled injection. Experiments ran on one NVIDIA A100 MIG-4g.40gb slice with bf16
weights.

\section{Composition-strength calibration}
\label{app:composition-strength}

Single-vector steering used $\alpha=4$, selected from a sweep over $\{2,4,6,8\}$ as the knee of the
single-behavior effect--coherence curve. For composition, we separately tuned the normalized-sum
scheme because fixing the total norm changes the effective per-direction push relative to
single-vector steering. On a five-pair pilot we swept $\alpha\in\{4,4.5,5,5.5,6\}$ and used the
utility $\Delta_{\mathrm{comp}}\cdot \mathrm{coherence}/100$ to balance behavioral movement against
coherence. The utility peaked at $\alpha=4.5$, with nearby values giving lower or comparable utility.
We therefore fixed $\alpha_{\mathrm{norm}}=4.5$ for the full normalized-sum run. The
projection-controlled scheme was not separately tuned; we set $\alpha_{\mathrm{axis}}=4.5$ to keep
the two controlled composition schemes directly comparable.

\section{Behavior set and selection}
\label{app:behaviors}

The final eight behaviors are \emph{apathetic}, \emph{confidence}, \emph{evil}, \emph{formality},
\emph{hallucinating}, \emph{humorous}, \emph{impolite}, and \emph{sycophantic}. Six
(\emph{apathetic}, \emph{evil}, \emph{hallucinating}, \emph{humorous}, \emph{impolite},
\emph{sycophantic}) come from the released Persona Vectors trait set. Two (\emph{confidence},
\emph{formality}) were generated with the same prompt-template procedure using \texttt{gpt-4.1}.

The candidate pool had $15$ traits: seven from the released set and eight project-generated traits.
Four traits failed individual-vector validation under the dual judge/log-probability protocol,
primarily because of RLHF baseline saturation or weak generated artifacts. Two had unresolved
judge--log-probability or polarity disagreements. One individually validated trait,
\emph{power-seeking}, was removed after a single-vector audit on the composition prompts revealed a
structural topic-bias problem in the trait judge. No pairwise composition outcomes were used to
choose the final trait set.

\section{Extraction details}
\label{app:extraction}

For each behavior, contrastive system prompts elicit behavior-positive and neutral responses to a
fixed bank of questions. A response is retained for vector construction only if it passes behavior
and coherence filters under the judge. Each activation entering Eq.~\ref{eq:direction} is the mean
residual stream over response tokens at $L^\star=17$. This follows the response-averaged
Persona Vectors construction rather than a single prompt-token construction.

\section{Judge mechanics and validation}
\label{app:judge}

We use \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} as the judge for extraction, validation, and composition scoring. For
each scored response, the judge receives the question and answer only; it does not see the steering
scheme, condition, pair partner, steering strength, or any method label. Trait and coherence scores
are obtained through separate calls. The score is the probability-weighted mean over integer tokens
from $0$ to $100$: the API is queried for one output token with log probabilities, and the final
score is the normalized expectation over tokens that parse as integers in the valid range. If
non-numeric tokens hold more than $75\%$ of the mass, the score is treated as missing.

Trait rubrics use the exact \texttt{eval\_prompt} stored for each trait. The coherence rubric is the
Persona Vectors coherence judge: $0$ means invalid or nonsensical English, $50$ means confused or
rambling, and $100$ means the answer is clearly coherent. The rubric instructs the judge to evaluate
coherence independently of trait expression and not to judge correctness.

\section{Coherence gate and censored pairs}
\label{app:coherence}

The coherence gate is applied separately for each behavior pair and composition scheme. For each
joint condition, we average coherence over its $100$ generations and retain that pair/scheme
observation if the mean coherence is at least $30$. The threshold was chosen after auditing
generations below $30$, where broken or repetitive text caused behavior over-attribution by the
judge. A stricter threshold of $50$ discards too much readable signal; threshold-sensitivity checks
preserve the qualitative directional result.

Under normalized-sum injection, no pairs are removed. Under projection-controlled injection, six of
$28$ pairs are removed:

Five of the six removed pairs have cosine at most $+0.13$, consistent with the total-norm blow-up of
projection-controlled injection for opposed directions.

\section{Additional statistical checks}
\label{app:stats}

The main paper reports exact node-permutation tests with controls for mean and maximum
single-vector strength. Bivariate associations are larger: for normalized-sum,
$r(c_{ij},M)=0.646$ and $r(c_{ij},S)=0.475$; for projection-controlled injection,
$r(c_{ij},M)=0.642$ and $r(c_{ij},S)=0.472$. These bivariate relationships are not confirmatory,
because they do not control for single-vector strength.

Adding a semantic-similarity control between behavior descriptions strengthens rather than removes
the directional cosine coefficient: normalized-sum increases from $+0.310$ to $+0.361$, and
projection-controlled from $+0.411$ to $+0.443$. Thus the directional association is not explained
by label-level semantic similarity.

Leave-one-behavior-out results show that the normalized-sum effect is sensitive to the behavior set:
dropping \emph{apathetic} reduces the coefficient to $+0.064$, while several other drops preserve a
positive coefficient. Projection-controlled estimates remain positive across folds, ranging from
$+0.246$ to $+0.549$, though this scheme is also affected by coherence censoring. These checks
support the main interpretation as a moderate behavior-set-dependent tendency rather than a sharp
rule.

\section{Naive unnormalized composition}
\label{app:naive}

We also audited an earlier unnormalized-sum baseline, which injects $\alpha(\hat v_i+\hat v_j)$.
Its total perturbation norm is $\alpha\sqrt{2+2c_{ij}}$, so high-cosine pairs receive a larger total
perturbation. This produces coherence collapse in high-cosine, over-dosed pairs and creates a
misleading relationship between geometry and discrete composition regimes. Because this analysis
used an earlier trait frame and is confounded by angle-dependent dose, it is treated as a diagnostic
failure mode rather than a confirmatory result.
